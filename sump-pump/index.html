Water rising in the basement? Act now

Stay out of standing water if outlets, panels or appliances are in the basement, since electricity and water do not mix. If it is safe, check that the pump is plugged in and that its float moves freely. A tripped GFCI outlet is a common cause of a dead pump.

Call us. We handle sump emergencies overnight and on weekends, with same-day appointments when available. Move valuables up, and if you have a wet-dry vacuum or a portable pump, use it to buy time.

Why sump pumps fail when you need them

Storms test every weak point.

Power loss.

Storms knock out electricity, and a plain pump stops with it.

Stuck float.

The float switch sticks against the pit wall or debris.

Clogged intake.

Silt and gravel jam the impeller.

Failed check valve.

Water falls back into the pit and the pump cycles constantly.

Burned-out motor.

Older pumps run dry or run too long and overheat.

Frozen discharge.

The outside line freezes or clogs and the pump cannot push water out.

Primary pump with or without backup

A second system matters in a long outage.

Primary pump only

  • Lower cost and simple.
  • Fine for a basement that rarely gets wet.
  • Stops when the power does.
  • No second chance in a big storm.

Primary plus battery backup.

  • Keeps pumping for hours without power.
  • Alarm warns you of a problem.
  • Higher upfront cost.
  • Best choice for finished basements.

Natick's water table and storms

Natick sits between Lake Cochituate, the Charles River and low areas that fill fast in a heavy rain. The National Weather Service recorded 7 to 10 inches of rain across eastern Massachusetts on March 13 to 15, 2010, and portions of Route 9 in Natick and Wellesley were shut down. Basements across MetroWest took on water in that event.

The practical lesson is to test your pump before spring. Pour a bucket of water in the pit and watch it start and empty. Also confirm where it discharges. Some nearby towns, including Framingham and Needham, treat sump water sent into the sewer as a violation, so ask the Natick Water and Sewer Division where yours should go.

How a sump pump install or replacement goes

Most are done in one visit.

1

Inspect the pit.

We check the pit size, liner and discharge route.

2

Remove the old pump.

Old check valves and piping come out.

3

Set the new pump.

The pump goes on a solid base with a clean float path.

4

Add a check valve and discharge.

A new check valve and a clear outlet are installed.

5

Test and add backup.

We run water through the pit and set up backup if you want one.

What drives sump pump cost

The pump is not the whole story.

Pump type. Submersible and pedestal pumps differ.
Backup system. Battery or water-powered backups add cost.
Discharge piping. A longer or rerouted discharge adds work.
The pit. A new pit or liner is separate.
Power. Adding a dedicated outlet is an electrician's job and we say so.
Pump, backup and discharge priced together

Insurance often excludes water that backs up or seeps in unless you carry a specific endorsement. Describe the pit and any past flooding, and we will price the pump with or without a backup.

Code, permits and licensing for this work

Sump pump installation involves plumbing and electrical work. The piping follows 248 CMR, the pump follows its manufacturer's guidelines and a dedicated GFCI-protected outlet is an electrician's job. Ask the Natick Water and Sewer Division where sump discharge is allowed to go.

Sources: 248 CMR, pump manufacturers' guidelines and the Natick Water and Sewer Division.
