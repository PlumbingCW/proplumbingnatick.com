What the record says about South Natick

The village has one of the longest histories in town. The Massachusetts Historical Commission's town survey says South Natick began as a Praying Town in 1650 and was built up more densely along Eliot and Pleasant Streets after 1775. By 1855 it had 65 houses. Later growth was small and stayed on a street grid southeast of the Eliot and Pleasant Street junction.

The John Eliot Historic District covers the center of the village. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places on June 23, 1983, spans about 71 acres, and includes buildings from the 1700s into the early 1900s, among them the Bacon Free Library of 1880. The survey also notes that Sudbury Aqueduct tunnels were driven beneath South Natick in 1874 to 1876.

What that means for you is simple. A house in or near the village can be a blend of eras, with additions and updates stacked over the years. We do not assume what is behind your walls. A plumber looks at the actual pipes, valves and drains first, then tells you what is there.

Sources: Wikipedia entry for the John Eliot Historic District citing the National Register file, and the 1980 Massachusetts Historical Commission Natick town report.

Test the pump before the storm

If your lot sits low near the river, the quiet time of year is the time to check your sump. Pour a bucket of water into the pit and watch the pump start, run and shut off. Make sure the discharge pipe carries water outdoors, away from the foundation.

Natick bills sewer use from the water meter, and the MWRA treats the town's sewage, so water that reaches the sewer costs money. Ask the Natick Water and Sewer Division at (508) 647-6557 where a sump discharge may go for your address.
