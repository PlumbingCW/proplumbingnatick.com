Why look before you fix

A sewer problem can look the same from the outside whether it is a grease clog, a root mass or a collapsed pipe. Each needs a different fix, and guessing wrong costs money. A camera inspection turns a guess into a picture.

You can watch the footage with the plumber and see the problem yourself. If the pipe is fine, that is useful too, because it rules out an expensive repair.

How an inspection works

Most inspections take a small part of the day.

1

Find an access point.

We use a cleanout, a pulled toilet or a roof vent.

2

Feed the camera.

The camera travels the line while a transmitter marks its location.

3

Record the footage.

You see pipe material, joints, cracks and debris on screen.

4

Mark the depth.

We note the depth and spot of any defect for planning.

5

Review together.

We explain what we found and what, if anything, to do next.

What the camera can show

Each finding points to a next step.

Buying an older Natick house

With about 45 percent of Natick homes built in 1959 or earlier, many sellers do not know what is under the lawn. Clay, cast iron and Orangeburg pipe all show up in older New England neighborhoods, and each has known weaknesses. A camera inspection before closing can reveal a lateral problem that a home inspector's visual check cannot.

If the camera finds a problem, you can negotiate before closing. For repair planning, see sewer line repair. For a recurring clog with no cause, the camera tells us whether cleaning will hold.

Good reasons to book an inspection

Plan one if any of these fit.

  • You are buying or selling a house with an older sewer lateral.
  • Drains back up more than once a year.
  • A sewer smell comes and goes in the yard or basement.
  • A big tree stands near the line.
  • You plan a remodel or an addition that touches the plumbing.
  • A previous repair left you wondering if it worked.

Code, permits and licensing for this work

A camera inspection follows common industry standards. The recording shows pipe material, joints and defects, and the locator marks depth. Anything repaired afterward is covered by 248 CMR, so keep the footage to support a permit conversation with the town.

Sources: industry camera-inspection practice and 248 CMR for follow-up repairs.
