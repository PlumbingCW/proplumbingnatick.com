How to Plumb a Bathtub in a Natick Home

A tub is three plumbing jobs in one: supply, drain and vent. Here is what each involves and where homeowners get into trouble.

Call (508) 389-4045

By the Natick Pro Plumbing team
Tub guide

Published 2026-10-08. Updated 2026-11-14.

Know the three systems

Plumbing a tub means connecting the fixtures to three systems. The supply brings hot and cold water to the valve. The drain carries used water away through a trap. The vent lets air into the drain so water flows freely. If any of the three is wrong, the tub fails in an annoying way.

Most of the work happens before the tub is set, because the drain and trap sit under the tub and are hard to reach afterward. Plan and test first.

What each part does

The order of work

Doing it in this order avoids rework.

1

Plan the layout.

Measure the tub, the wall studs and the drain location.

2

Rough in the drain and vent.

Set the drain pipe with a steady slope and connect the vent.

3

Set the tub.

Place the tub on a solid base and connect the drain and overflow.

4

Rough in the supply.

Install the valve at the right depth and run hot and cold lines.

5

Test before closing walls.

Pressure test the supply and run water through the drain.

Slope, venting and why old houses surprise you

Drains need a steady downward slope so water carries solids with it. Too steep and water outruns the solids, too flat and it stalls. In older houses, the existing drain may be cast iron or galvanized pipe with a rough interior, and a new tub tied to it can inherit its problems.

Venting is the other surprise. Older bathrooms may share a vent with another fixture in ways that no longer meet current practice. Our plumbing installation page explains how we handle rough-in in older homes.

What is special about a Natick tub install

Natick's 2025 water report lists hardness around 108 parts per million, which is enough to leave scale on tub spouts and shower valves. Choose a valve with removable cartridges and plan an access panel so the valve can be serviced later.

About 45 percent of Natick homes were built in 1959 or earlier, according to the town's 2025 community profile. Bathrooms in those houses often have narrow framing and original drains. Check what is behind the wall before you pick the tub size.

Source: Town of Natick 2025 Consumer Confidence Report and 2025 community profile.

When to hire a plumber

Call us if any of these apply.

  • You are moving the drain or adding a new bathroom.
  • The tub is going on a slab or basement floor.
  • The existing drain is cast iron or galvanized and in poor shape.
  • You need a permit or an inspection.
  • You are working with a heavy cast iron or stone tub.
  • You want a shower valve moved or replaced.

Choosing a tub type

Alcove tubs sit between three walls and are the simplest to plumb because the drain is at one end. Freestanding tubs need floor-mounted supply and a drain that lines up with the floor opening, which usually means planning the framing ahead. Cast iron tubs are heavy and need a solid floor. Acrylic tubs are lighter but need a full base so they do not flex and crack.

Whichever you choose, read the fixture instructions before the rough-in. Drain location and overflow style differ, and the wrong guess means opening the floor again.

Testing before you close the wall

Fill the tub to the overflow and let it sit for an hour, checking the drain connection below for drips. Run the supply under pressure and look at every joint. Then run water down the drain and watch for slow flow or gurgling. Taking an hour to test costs far less than patching tile, and it is the step most people skip.

Take photos of the pipes before the wall is closed. A good picture of the valve location and the drain run is worth more than memory when you need to fix something years later.

Common leak points on a new tub

Three spots cause most call-backs. The first is the drain and overflow gasket, which leaks if it is not seated or if the fastener is overtightened. The second is the trap under the floor, where a slip joint can weep if it is not aligned. The third is the valve trim, where a missing seal lets water behind the wall.

Each of these is cheap to fix before the wall is closed and expensive after. If you ever notice a stain on the ceiling below a bathroom, treat it as a tub leak until proven otherwise and call us.

Tools and materials list

A typical tub install needs a level, a tape measure and pipe cutters. You also need a drain kit, a trap, drain and vent pipe with fittings, and a valve and trim kit. Plumber's putty or silicone seals the drain flange, and you need a way to test pressure. A reciprocating saw and a drill make framing changes easier. Wear eye protection and gloves, and keep the work area clear so you do not trip on pipe.

Permits and licenses in Massachusetts

Massachusetts plumbing work falls under 248 CMR, and the state licenses plumbers through its Board of State Examiners of Plumbers and Gas Fitters. Ask the Natick building department about permits before you open walls. If you hire, check the plumber's license on the state's lookup page.
