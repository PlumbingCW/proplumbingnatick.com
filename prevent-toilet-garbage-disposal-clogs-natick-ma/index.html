How to Prevent Toilet and Garbage Disposal Clogs in Natick

Most clogs are preventable. The rules are short and the payoff is a drain that works when you need it.

Prevention guide

Published 2026-11-08. Updated 2026-11-14.

Two fixtures, one idea

Toilets and garbage disposals look different but fail the same way. Something goes in that the pipe cannot carry. A little discipline at the source avoids the plunger, the plumber and the cleanup.

The lists below are short on purpose. Post them where the family can see them.

Toilet: yes and no

Only waste and toilet paper belong in a toilet.

Okay to flush

  • Human waste.
  • Toilet paper.
  • Nothing else, as a rule.
  • A small amount of paper is fine.

Never flush.

  • Wipes, even if the label says flushable.
  • Cotton balls, swabs and dental floss.
  • Feminine products and diapers.
  • Paper towels, tissues and cat litter.

Disposal: yes and no

A disposal is a food chopper, not a trash can.

Okay to grind

  • Small soft food scraps.
  • A little citrus rind.
  • Run cold water while it runs.
  • Let it finish before turning off water.

Never grind.

  • Grease, fat and oil.
  • Coffee grounds in quantity.
  • Pasta, rice and potato peels.
  • Bones, fruit pits and fibrous stalks.

A Natick view on what goes down the drain

Natick's water and sewer division says sewer charges follow your water meter, and the MWRA treats the town's sewage. A clog that backs into your house is one problem. Wipes and grease that make it to the sewer add to blockages and equipment trouble at a regional level.

More than 45 percent of Natick's homes were built before 1960, according to the town's 2025 community profile. Older drains have rough walls and small diameters, and they catch what newer pipe lets pass. If you live in an older house, be stricter about what you flush.

Source: Natick Water and Sewer Division FAQ and 2025 community profile.

Disposal care

A disposal lasts longer with a little care. Run cold water before, during and after use. Grind small amounts at a time. Occasional ice cubes and citrus peels can freshen the chamber. If it jams, turn it off and unplug it before you free the blades. For a disposal that hums but will not spin, see our garbage disposal repair page.

Keep in mind that disposals do not remove the need to scrape plates. A drain line that handles a lot of food waste needs cleaning more often.

Teaching the household

Prevention works only if everyone follows it. A small sign in the bathroom and one near the kitchen sink is enough. Tell guests and babysitters. Keep a wastebasket next to every toilet so the right choice is the easy one.

Children are the main source of toy-and-wipe surprises, so a quick talk about what the toilet is for goes a long way. For kitchens, a small compost bin next to the sink gives scraps a better place to go than the drain.

Cleaning a disposal without chemicals

Grind a few ice cubes with a handful of rock salt to knock film off the blades, then run cold water. Follow with a few citrus peels for a fresh smell. Skip bleach and strong cleaners, which can harm seals and rubber parts and are unpleasant to splash.

If you notice a persistent odor, turn the disposal off, unplug it and clean the underside of the splash guard with a small brush. Food builds up there and is the cause of most smells.

Signs a clog is building

Slow flushing, a gurgle after a flush and a sink that drains a little slower each week all signal a clog in progress. Catching it early is the cheap fix. A plunger or a hot flush now can avoid a backup later.

If a toilet clogs more than a couple of times a month, ask why. The cause could be the toilet, the drain line or a habit. A plumber can tell you which, and our toilet service page covers when to repair and when to replace.

A weekly and monthly routine

Weekly, flush hot water through the kitchen and bathroom drains and clean hair from tub catchers. Monthly, clean the disposal splash guard, rinse the sink strainer and check under sinks for leaks. Twice a year, test the shutoff valves under every sink and toilet.

Fifteen minutes a month costs nothing and keeps small problems from growing. It also gives you a chance to spot a leak before it stains a ceiling.

When prevention is not enough

Some clogs come from the pipe, not the habits. Roots, a sag or an old cast iron line can catch even well-behaved waste. If your toilet clogs often even when you use little paper, a toilet service call may find a flush problem, and a camera look can find a line problem. We also help in Framingham and Ashland.
