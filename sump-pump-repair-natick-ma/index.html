Sump Pump Repair in Natick: What to Check Before You Call

A sump pump that fails in a storm is a bad night. These checks take a few minutes and often find the cause.

Sump guide

Published 2026-11-04. Updated 2026-11-14.

Start with the simple stuff

Most sump pump failures are not mysterious. The pump has no power, the float is stuck, the intake is clogged or the discharge is frozen. Each takes minutes to check. If you can fix it quickly, you save a service call. If you cannot, the notes help us fix it faster.

Safety first. Never touch a pump, cord or outlet while standing in water. If the basement is wet and the outlet is live, stay out and call us.

Symptom, cause and quick check

Match the symptom first.

SymptomLikely causeQuick check
Pump does nothing.No power or tripped GFCI.Reset the outlet and test with a lamp.
Pump runs but no water leaves.Clogged impeller or frozen discharge.Check the discharge line outside.
Pump will not shut off.Stuck float.Lift the float and free it from the wall.
Pump cycles constantly.Failed check valve or small pit.Look for water flowing back into the pit.
Loud grinding.Worn motor or debris.Unplug and inspect.
Pump runs but basement still wet.Water sources beyond the pit.Check for leaks and drain backup.

A plumber can handle the rest.

A five-minute test routine

Run it twice a year.

1

Check the outlet.

Press the GFCI reset, then confirm the cord is plugged in.

2

Pour in water.

Add a bucket of water to the pit.

3

Watch the float.

It should lift and start the pump.

4

Listen.

The pump should run smoothly and empty the pit.

5

Check the outside discharge.

Water should flow away from the foundation.

Natick storms and sump pumps

According to the National Weather Service, 7 to 10 inches of rain fell over eastern Massachusetts in March 2010, portions of Route 9 in Natick and Wellesley closed and the Sudbury River at Saxonville hit a record crest. That pattern of long, heavy rain is exactly what tests a sump pump. Pumps that run for hours wear out and power often fails with the storm.

A battery backup is the practical response. Some towns near Natick, including Framingham and Needham, say sump discharge into the sewer is a problem, so ask the Natick Water and Sewer Division where yours should go.

Sources: National Weather Service March 2010 summary and town inflow and infiltration pages.

Repair or replace the pump

Age and condition decide.

Repair

  • Good for a stuck float or a failed check valve.
  • Quick and low cost.
  • Pump is under about seven years.
  • Motor and impeller are sound.

Replace.

  • Better for a burned-out motor or heavy wear.
  • A new pump gives peace of mind.
  • Chance to add a backup.
  • See our sump pump service page.

Discharge problems

Where the water goes matters.

Frozen outlet

In deep cold, the outside pipe can freeze solid. A pipe that slopes and ends in a free-draining spot helps.

Discharge too close

Water that drains next to the foundation soaks back in. Extend the pipe away from the house.

Discharge into the sewer

This is not allowed in many towns and adds load to the sewer. Ask the town before you connect.

When to call

Call if the pump does not start after you check power and float, if the basement is flooding or if you hear grinding. If you are in Wayland or Framingham, where river-side lots are common, test the pump ahead of every spring.

We take sump emergencies at any hour. For longer-term fixes, our basement waterproofing page explains the options.

Float switches explained

The float switch tells the pump when to start and stop. Vertical floats ride a rod and can jam against the pit wall. Tethered floats swing on a cord and can tangle with the discharge pipe. Electronic switches have no moving float but need a clean sensor.

Most float problems come from a pit that is too narrow or a pump that has shifted. Resetting the pump on a flat base often fixes it. If the float still sticks, replacing the switch is cheaper than replacing the pump.

Power, backups and the storm that takes both

The worst timing for a sump failure is a storm, because the same storm knocks out power. A battery backup runs the pump for several hours depending on the battery and how often the pump cycles. It also sounds an alarm to warn you.

A water-powered backup uses municipal pressure instead of a battery and suits homes on public water, but it uses water while it runs and needs a check valve, so we explain the trade-offs before you choose.

What a repair visit includes

We check power, the float, the impeller and the check valve. We test the discharge line and look at the pit for silt, debris and cracks. We run water through the pit to watch a full cycle and listen for noise. If the pump is worn, we tell you whether repair or replacement makes sense, and we mention a backup if the basement has flooded before.

You get a plain explanation of what we found and a clear price before we change anything.

Keep it working

Test the pump twice a year. Clean the pit of silt and debris. Check the check valve for slime. Replace a pump that is a decade old before it fails. Keep the discharge pipe clear. A maintenance habit costs little and avoids a flooded basement, and the best moment to do it is before the wet season, not during it.
