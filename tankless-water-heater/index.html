Is tankless right for your house?

A tankless heater heats water as it flows, so it never runs out the way a tank can. That suits a busy household, a big soaking tub or a basement bath far from the main unit. It does not suit every house, and we will say so if yours is one of them.

The deciding factors are gas capacity, venting and your water quality. Natick's 2025 water report lists hardness near 108 parts per million, which is moderate but enough to scale a heat exchanger without flushing.

Before you switch from a tank

Two paths, each with trade-offs.

Stay with a tank

  • Lower upfront cost.
  • Fits the existing gas line and flue.
  • Simple to repair for years.
  • Hot water can run out during heavy use.

Go tankless.

  • Endless hot water at the flow rate it is sized for.
  • Takes less wall and floor space.
  • May need a larger gas line and new venting.
  • Needs yearly flushing to stay efficient.

Common tankless problems we fix

Most issues come down to scale, ignition, flow or venting.

Error codes.

Codes point to ignition, flame, venting or sensor problems. We read the code and test the cause.

Cold water sandwich.

A blast of cold water between hot bursts. Often a flow, sensor or setup issue.

Low flow or weak heat.

Scale on the heat exchanger or clogged inlet screens.

Shutting off mid-shower.

Flame failure, blocked venting or low gas pressure.

Noise or fluttering.

Scale buildup or a gas supply restriction.

Leaks at fittings.

Isolation valves, filters or the condensate line.

How a descaling flush works

Yearly flushing is the single best habit for a tankless unit.

1

Isolate the unit.

We close the water and gas, then connect hoses to the service valves.

2

Circulate descaler.

A food-safe descaling solution runs through the heat exchanger.

3

Rinse thoroughly.

Clean water flushes out the loosened scale and residue.

4

Clean the filters.

The inlet screen is cleaned and the unit is tested.

Installing in an older Natick house

Many older homes, especially those built before 1960, have half-inch gas lines meant for a stove and a small tank. A tankless unit may draw several times that gas volume. Upsizing a gas line is a real cost, and we price it up front, never as a surprise.

Venting is the other question. Some basements have a masonry chimney that a tankless unit cannot use as is. Newer vents run through the side wall, which means planning the route. For a planned install, our installation team handles the permit conversation and the fuel work together.

What affects tankless cost

The unit is a smaller share than people expect.

Capacity. Bigger households need larger units.
Gas line work. Upsizing is a common add-on.
Venting. New side-wall vents take parts and labor.
Condensate and drains. High-efficiency units need a place to send acidic condensate.
Water treatment. A softener or scale filter protects the unit.

Code, permits and licensing for this work

Tankless units are installed to the manufacturer's guidelines for gas sizing, venting and condensate, along with the state plumbing code, 248 CMR. Because gas capacity and venting are the common failure points, ask for the sizing calculation in writing before work begins.

Sources: 248 CMR and tankless manufacturers' sizing and venting guidelines.
