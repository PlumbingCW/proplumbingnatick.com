Copper Pipe to PEX in a Natick Home: Should You Switch?

PEX has become the default for new homes. Here is how it compares to copper and what a switch involves in an older Natick house.

Call (508) 389-4045

By the Natick Pro Plumbing team
PEX guide

Published 2026-10-26. Updated 2026-11-14.

Why people ask

Copper has served homes for decades. PEX, a flexible cross-linked polyethylene tubing, now shows up in most new construction. People ask about switching for three reasons. A copper line has pinholes, the house is being remodeled or a plumber suggested it. The right answer depends on which reason applies.

This guide compares the two honestly and explains where each one shines.

Side by side

Both work. They differ in how they install and age.

FeatureCopperPEX
Flexibility.Rigid, needs many fittings.Bends around corners.
Freeze tolerance.Can split.Expands slightly and often survives.
Joining.Solder or press fittings.Crimp, clamp or expansion fittings.
Sunlight.Not affected.Must be protected from UV.
Heat.Excellent.Rated for hot water but not for direct heat sources.
Installation time.Longer.Often shorter.

Neither is universally better. Fit the material to the job.

Natick conditions that matter

Natick's 2025 water quality report lists copper at 0.36 parts per million at the 90th percentile, with none of 30 sampled homes over the action level, and hardness around 108 parts per million. The water is not unusually aggressive, but the town's own report does not describe corrosivity, so we do not claim your copper will or will not pit.

What the town's housing data tells us is that 16 percent of homes were built between 1960 and 1979 and 22 percent between 1980 and 1999. Those decades are when copper plumbing was standard, so many Natick homes have copper that is fifty years old or more.

Source: Town of Natick 2025 Consumer Confidence Report and 2025 community profile.

Full conversion or partial

The scope is your call.

Partial conversion

  • Replace only the failing run.
  • Smaller project and lower cost.
  • Mixed materials need proper transition fittings.
  • Works when the rest of the pipe is sound.

Full conversion.

  • Replace all supply lines.
  • Removes the uncertainty of old copper.
  • More openings in walls and ceilings.
  • See our repiping page for how we plan it.

Questions people forget to ask

A few practical details.

Is PEX allowed where I live

PEX is widely accepted, but plumbing in Massachusetts follows 248 CMR. Ask the Natick building department about permits, and ask your plumber about current practice.

What about the water heater connection

PEX should not connect directly to a water heater without proper metal transitions. Your plumber will install the correct fittings.

Does PEX affect taste

Some people notice a slight taste in new PEX. It fades after a flush. Run the lines for a bit after installation.

Signs a conversion makes sense

Consider it if you see these.

  • Repeated pinhole leaks in copper in different spots.
  • A planned remodel that opens walls anyway.
  • Freeze splits in exposed lines.
  • Mixed old materials that need updating.
  • A desire to cut installation time and wall damage.

What a conversion does to your walls

PEX is flexible, so it can be pulled through existing holes in framing in many cases. That means fewer and smaller openings than a rigid copper replacement would need. Still, any repipe opens some drywall, usually at access points near fixtures, in closets and along basement ceilings.

A good plumber plans the route before cutting anything, marks the openings for your approval and patches them to be paint-ready. Ask for a written plan so there are no surprises, and ask who does the finish work.

Noise, pressure and comfort

Many homeowners ask whether PEX is quieter. It often is, because it absorbs some of the water hammer that rings through copper. Pressure and flow depend on the pipe size and the layout, not just the material, so a good design matters more than the brand of tubing.

Hot water delivery can also improve if the new layout shortens the run to a distant bathroom. If your hot water takes a long time to reach one fixture, mention it when we plan the work, since a shorter path or a recirculation loop may help.

Cost drivers without the guesswork

Cost depends on the number of fixtures, the length of runs, how much wall must open and how much finish work you want included. A small bath conversion is a day or less. A whole house is several days and involves more coordination. We give you a clear price before work starts, and we show you what is and is not included.

Ask any contractor whether the quote covers patching, painting, permits and disposal. Surprises usually hide in the items nobody asked about.

When to hold off

If one short run failed and the rest of the copper is sound, patching may be better. A plumber can show you the evidence: the color and thickness of the pipe, the number of leaks and the age. We would rather tell you not to convert than sell a job you do not need. Our leak detection work often helps decide.

If you live in Needham or Wellesley, where a lot of homes date from the same decades, a quick survey of a few pipes can tell you a lot.
