Water Heater Anode Rod Replacement in Natick, MA

A sacrificial rod inside your tank fights corrosion so the steel does not have to. Here is how to check it and why it matters.

Anode guide

What the anode rod does

Inside a steel tank water heater hangs a rod made of magnesium or aluminum. It corrodes on purpose so the tank wall does not. When the rod is gone, the tank starts rusting. The rod is the cheapest part of the heater and one of the most neglected.

Checking it every few years can add years to the tank. Replacing a spent rod is far cheaper than a new heater, and it is the kind of maintenance that makes a tank last.

Magnesium or aluminum

The two common rod materials suit different water.

Magnesium

  • Protects the tank well in most water.
  • Wears faster, so it needs checking.
  • Common in factory installs.
  • Can smell in some water.

Aluminum.

  • Lasts longer in many cases.
  • Good where water is hard.
  • Not suited to every household.
  • Ask a plumber about the aluminum content.

What Natick's water means for the rod

Natick's 2025 water quality report lists hardness around 108 parts per million, about 6.3 grains per gallon, and a mix of groundwater sources. That is moderate. In moderately hard water, the rod usually wears at a steady pace, and sediment collects at the bottom of the tank.

The report did not include a corrosivity statement, so the town's data does not tell us how fast your rod will wear. Check it at the interval your manual lists and note how much of the rod is left. The wear rate in your own tank is the real answer.

Source: Town of Natick 2025 Consumer Confidence Report.

How to check the rod

A basic check takes less than an hour.

1

Turn off power or fuel.

Switch off the breaker or set the gas control to off.

2

Close the cold supply.

Shut the valve above the tank and relieve pressure at a hot tap.

3

Drain a few gallons.

Lower the water level below the rod's port at the top.

4

Loosen the rod.

Use a socket and a breaker bar. Rods are often stuck tight.

5

Inspect.

If the rod is thinner than a pencil in spots or the core wire shows, replace it.

Common problems

The job is simple but has traps.

A stuck rod

Rods can be locked in place by corrosion. Using the wrong tool can break the port. A plumber has the leverage and the experience.

Low clearance

Many tanks sit in closets with little overhead room. A flexible rod can be installed in those spaces.

An old tank

If the heater is over a decade old and was never serviced, a new rod may not save it. We tell you honestly, and our replacement page explains when to switch.

What else to do during the visit

Pair the rod check with these.

  • Flush sediment from the bottom of the tank.
  • Test the relief valve for proper operation.
  • Check the temperature setting.
  • Inspect connections and the vent for corrosion.
  • Note the age and the warranty paperwork.

When replacement does not make sense

A tank that is already leaking from the body or rusting heavily needs replacing. A new rod cannot repair holes. If your tank leaks from the seam or the bottom, see our repair page for how we diagnose, and plan a swap.

If you are in Wellesley, where hardness varies a lot between water sources, rod wear can differ from street to street. Ask the town which source feeds yours.

A cheap part that protects an expensive tank

Replacing a spent anode rod costs a fraction of a new water heater. A rod that has been working for years has sacrificed itself to protect the steel, which is its entire job. When it is gone, the water attacks the tank lining, and pinhole leaks follow. The tank rarely gives much notice.

That is why checking the rod is one of the best habits a homeowner can build. Spending an afternoon with a socket and a breaker bar once every few years can add years to a tank that would otherwise go to the curb.

Tools, torque and a stuck rod

Most anode rods use a one and one-sixteenth inch hex head on top of the tank. A long breaker bar and a six-point socket give the best grip. An impact wrench can help, but avoid cross-threading when you reinstall. Wrap the threads of the new rod with plumber's tape designed for water, not gas, and snug it firmly without cranking.

If the rod will not budge, stop. Forcing it risks twisting the tank fitting. Soaking the threads with penetrating oil and giving it time sometimes helps. If not, a plumber has longer leverage and, if needed, a flexible rod for low ceilings.

Keeping a simple maintenance log

Write the date on the tank each time you flush it or check the rod, and note what you found. A log turns guesswork into a pattern. If your rod is half gone after two years, you know to check it more often. If it looks nearly new after five, you can relax a little.

How often to check

According to many manufacturer guidelines, checking every few years is a reasonable interval. Heavily used heaters and hard water call for more frequent checks. Mark the date on the tank so you do not have to guess. Treat the check as part of an annual routine, along with a short flush.

A little maintenance goes a long way. A heater that is looked after in a moderately hard water area like Natick often outlasts one that is ignored, and a plumber's quick look takes a fraction of the time of a replacement.
